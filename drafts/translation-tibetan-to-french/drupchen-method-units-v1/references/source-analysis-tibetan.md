# Reading the Tibetan: what is the language and what is the author

From Drupchen, *Two strategies for improving machine translation from Tibetan* (2026), §§1–3 and §5, and *Subtracting the Typological Baseline* (2026), §7, CC BY 4.0. Summarised and reformatted. This side of the method does not depend on the target language.

## 1. The context sheet

Answer these five questions before any draft. Restate the answers at the top of every later turn.

| Question | What it decides |
| --- | --- |
| Target language. Is a pivot language used, and for what? | A pivot for understanding can stay rough; a target for readers must be finished. |
| Reader and register | Which models of good prose apply, and whether oral features are assets or noise. |
| How the source was delivered: interpreted consecutively, dictated to a scribe, composed in writing | Which features exist only because of how the source was produced, with an addressee who is not there in the target. |
| Target format: continuous prose, subtitles, a critical edition | Whether a function moves to layout (headings, numbering) and what re-segmentation is allowed. |
| Who decides terms | Whether a term choice is yours or must be looked up in the glossary. |

## 2. Sort every feature before you translate it

Put each source feature in one of four bins:

1. **Typological default** (a property of Tibetan, not a choice of the author). Do not preserve it, and do not count its absence as a loss.
2. **Genre convention** (for example the fixed slot order of a colophon). Re-encode it in the target genre's own convention.
3. **Marked choice** (something the author did when the language allowed another way). Carry its function across.
4. **Function discharged outside the text** (it served someone who is not there in the target, such as the interpreter in the room). Leave it out, or move it to layout.

The following are properties of Tibetan or of how it was produced, not assertions by the author. **Do not preserve them, and do not treat their absence from the target as loss:** one finite verb per period; verb-final position; context before predicate; repetition for durativity, iteration or intensification; concrete predication of abstract processes; flat juxtaposition linked by དེ་ནས་; ན་ as consecutive rather than hypothetical; segment-final ཟེར་ used to hand off a turn.

Real prominence devices in Tibetan are deviations from the default: a topic particle, the repeated-verb envelope (a nominalised verb opens a period and its finite form closes it), an unexpected subordinator. Those are marked.

Where the source speaks its own outline (དེ་ལ་གསུམ།, then དང་པོ་ … གཉིས་པ་ …) and the target format shows structure by layout: render it as text when the division asserts a relation and the author's terms do work; turn it into numbering when it only marks a position. Say which you did.

## 3. Meaning units

A meaning unit is one thing the passage asserts: an action, an agent, a condition, a quality, a named term. List the units as a flat numbered list **in plain language, with no source syntax**. For each unit, give its channel: `text` (must appear in the translation), `outside` (may be carried by layout) or `absent` (correctly left out, bin 4).

## 4. Flags: a closed list

Do not "flag what you are unsure of". That produces noise and misses the real errors. Mark **every** instance of these, whatever your confidence:

1. A zero subject or object whose referent you had to supply: name the referent you chose and the other possible one.
2. A clause whose argument structure rests on one ས-class particle (གི་/གིས་, ཀྱི་/ཀྱིས་, གྱི་/གྱིས་, འི་/ས་, འདིའི་/འདིས་): say which reading you took and what the other gives. This marks where the transcription matters, not uncertainty.
3. A ན་ clause: which of the five readings you took (conditional, relative, temporal, causal, manner).
4. A terminal ཟེར་མཁན་ or དེ་རེད་: confirm the stretch before it was read as a definition, not as an instruction.
5. A doublet or a repetition: collapsed or kept, and why.
6. An epistemic or modal particle: read literally or idiomatically.
7. A term not in the glossary: the one rendering you chose, used throughout.

Do not flag: additions the target syntax requires, readings you are in fact confident about, or anything repaired as oral disfluency.

Example of a type 2 flag:

> དགྲ་འདིའི་བུ་ — read as genitive, "a child of this enemy"; if agentive, "this enemy, born as a child", which reverses which party is the parent.

## 5. Terms

One rendering per term per file. No alternatives inline. When a term's rendering depends on position (for example ཞེ་སྡང་ inside the three poisons and inside the ཆགས་སྡང་ pair), state the rule. Transliteration follows the glossary.
